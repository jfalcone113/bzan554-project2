   There are other variations of Gradient Descent incorporating ideas to
   allow faster convergence to the optimum. The most popular algorithms
   are:
     ▶ A global (same for all parameters) and constant (across learning

         ▶ SGD with Momentum
         ▶ Nestorov’s Accelerated Gradient (NAG)
    ▶ A global (same for all parameters) and adaptive (across learning
      updates) learning rate
         ▶ Learning rate scheduling
    ▶ A local (per-parameter) adaptive (across learning updates) learning
      rate
         ▶ Adaptive gradient (AdaGrad)
         ▶ RMSprop
         ▶ Adam

Momentum
  SGD has trouble navigating plateaus. A plateau is common when
  variables have different scales (for example the elongated bowl in the
  figure happens because the feature representing the horizontal axis has
  much larger values than the one representing the y-axis)




  Picture a cost function that goes down steeply, then almost plateaus with
  a near-horizontal downward slope and then goes down steeply again. On
  the steep regions SGD will go fast, and on the horizontal region SGD will
  go very slowly. When momentum is added the plateau is traversed much
  faster.

Momentum
  This technique (https://arxiv.org/pdf/1609.04747) modifies the
  standard SGD by introducing velocity ν, and momentum γ which tries to
  control the velocity and prevents overshooting valleys while allowing
  faster descent as well as giving Gradient Descent a short-term memory.


                          νt = γνt−1 + (1 − γ)∇θ J(θ)
                                                                              (1)
                           θ = θ − ηνt

  The momentum term γ is usually set to 0.9. Summarizing, the
  momentum algorithm replaces the gradient with a weighted moving
  average of the gradient and uses that to update the weights. It therefore
  memorizes how fast we were descending before and uses that speed to
  traverse plateaus. A way to think about this is a ball that rolls down a
  curve. If it hits a perfectly horizontal plateau it will continue rolling for a
  while until it has lost all its velocity. In plain vanilla SGD it would have
  stopped immediately when hitting the plateau.

Nestorov’s Accelerated Gradient
    ▶ We would like our ball to look ahead and slow down if the loss is
      going to go up. Nesterov’s Accelerated Gradient is a small variation
      of momentum.
    ▶ Instead of calculating the gradient at the current position θ, we
      calculate the gradient at the approximate position that we will be in.
      Which information do we have (no additional computations) that we
      can add to θ before we compute the gradient? We know for sure
      that we are going to subtract ηγνt−1 from θ!
    ▶ From a physical perspective, it makes sense to make judgments
      about our position based on the position that we know that we are
      going to be in a short while.

                   νt = γνt−1 + (1 − γ)∇θ J(θ − ηγνt−1 )
                                                                        (2)
                    θ = θ − ηνt

Per parameter adaptive learning rate



    ▶ Until now we have used a global and equal learning rate for all our
      parameters (momentum and nesterov) and a global and adaptive
      learning rate (learning schedule).
    ▶ But what if we could speed up or slow down learning by increasing
      or decreasing this factor, even for each parameter, as the training
      progresses?
    ▶ We could adaptively tune learning and know into which direction to
      accelerate and decelerate. Several methods that use such adaptive
      learning rates have been proposed, most notably AdaGrad, RMSprop
      and ADAM.

AdaGrad
   ▶ GD is guaranteed to reach the minimum loss given convexity as long
     as we reduce the learning rate as epochs roll on.
   ▶ For infrequently occurring features (i.e., they are zero a lot) we may
     get into a situation where we have reduced the learning rate too
     quickly. We can’t just slow down the reduction in learning rate,
     because the frequently occurring features will then be jumping
     around their minima.
   ▶ We could keep a counter of how many times a feature was non-zero
     and divide the learning rate by this number. The learning rate for
     infrequently occurring features will therefore be higher than the
     learning rate for frequently occurring features.
   ▶ This fails whenever we do not quite have sparsity but rather just
     data where the gradients are often very small and only rarely large.
     After all, it is unclear where one would draw the line between
     something that qualifies as an observed feature or not (if features
     are small values; nearly zero; the updates will be small too).
   ▶ The solution is to take into account the sum of the historical
     gradients when we divide the learning rate.

AdaGrad
                      gt = gt−1 + (∇θt J(θt ))2
                                       η                                    (3)
                    θt+1 = θt − p                ∇θt J(θt )
                                  diag (gt ) + ϵ
   ▶ AdaGrad adapts the learning rate to the parameters by dividing the
     learning rate by the square root of the sum of squares of historic
     gradients. In the denominator
        ▶ the squares are taken to abstract away the sign of the gradients, and
          the root is taken to undo the effect of squaring and,
        ▶ the sum of the previous gradients is taken so that smaller updates
          (smaller learning rates) will be used for parameters associated with
          frequently occurring features, and vice versa for parameters
          associated with infrequently occurring features.
   ▶ gt is a column vector of length equal to the number of parameters,
     where an element is the sum of squares of the historic gradients up
     to iteration t for a parameter.
   ▶ diag (gt ) ∈ R d×d is a diagonal matrix (to matrix multiply with the
     gradient vector) where each diagonal element is the sum of the
     squares of the gradients w.r.t. θi up to iteration t and ϵ (say 10−8 )
     is a smoothing term to avoid division by zero                                28 / 105

===== PDF PAGE 29 =====
AdaGrad

  Summary:
   ▶ The learning rate is updated automatically at each time step t
   ▶ The learning rate is different per parameter
  Pros:
   ▶ Speeds up training
   ▶ Less need to manually tune learning rate
  Cons:
   ▶ For each parameter a sum is computed of the squared historic
     gradients and stored in gt . Since each element is positive, the sum
     keeps growing during training, eventually shrinking the learning rate
     to an infinitesimally small number, and thereby making weight
     updates impossible.

RMSprop: Root Mean Square Prop

   ▶ A disadvantage of AdaGrad is that the gt part of the update is
     monotonically increasing. This can pose problems because the
     learning rate can steadily decrease to the point where it stops the
     learning altogether.
   ▶ RMSprop solves this problem by dividing the learning rate by a
     running average of squared gradients. For example, if γ = 0.9 then
     the first gradient will be 0.9*g1 in iteration 1, 0.9*(0.9*g1 ) in
     iteration 2, 0.9*(0.9*(0.9*g1 )) in iteration 3, . . .
   ▶ Here γ is called the decay rate with a typical value of 0.999.
   ▶ RMSprop update:

                       gt = γgt−1 + (1 − γ)(∇θt J(θt ))2

Adaptive Moment Estimation (ADAM)

   ▶ Adam is the state-of-the-art first order optimization algorithm. It is
     an improvement upon RMSprop by adding momentum to the update
     rule, combining the best of both momentum and adaptive learning.
   ▶ ADAM stores the running average of past squared gradients gt2 like
     RMSprop, and the running average of past gradients vt like
     momentum
                       gt = γ1 gt−1 + (1 − γ1 )(∇θt J(θt ))2
                                                                        (5)
                       vt = γ2 vt−1 + (1 − γ2 )∇θt J(θt )

      The moving average vt is the first raw (around 0) moment (the
      mean) of the gradients and the moving average gt is the second raw
      moment (the variance) of the gradients. The hyper-parameters γ1
      (default: 0.9) and γ2 (default: 0.999) are decay rates controlling the
      weighted averages. t represents the iteration number starting at 1.

Vanishing/Exploding Gradients Problem

   What are the implications for deep networks?
    ▶ Forward pass: If we assume a funnel shaped network (subsequent
      layers each have less neurons) then more shallow layers will saturate
      sooner.
    ▶ Backward pass: weights going into more shallow layers receive
      increasingly small updates with every layer as we keep on having to
                   ∂h
      multiply by ∂z h
                       .
   This is what is called the vanishing gradient problem. It vanishes as we
   do our backward pass towards more shallow layers, and the reason is that
   neurons in more shallow layers have increasingly saturated outputs
   (where the slope is close to zero). The reason why they have increasingly
   saturated outputs is because more elements need to be added, each
   having a variance, and the variance of the sum is the sum of the
   variances. More variance means that we are working in the limits of the
   tanh and sigmoid functions.

Solution 3: Batch Normalization
    ▶ He initialization with ELU (or variants of RELU) can help us avoid
      problems at the beginning of learning. Batch normalization helps us
      stay out of trouble during learning.
    ▶ Batch Normalization (BN), introduced in 2015 by Ioffe and Szegedy
      to address the vanishing/exploding gradient problem, is a widely
      used technique for normalizing input.
    ▶ BN accommodates the Internal Covariate Shift: the distribution of
      each layer’s input changes during training as the parameters of
      previous layers change. This is a problem because a change in
      the distributions of layers’ inputs requires the layers to
      continuously adapt to the new distribution.
    ▶ BN involves adding a normalization operation right before or after
      the activation function of each hidden layer. This operation
      subtracts from each input variable the mini-batch mean µB of the
      input variable and divides by the mini-batch standard deviation σB
      of the input, where B denotes the mini-batch.


                                                                            63 / 105

===== PDF PAGE 64 =====
Batch Normalization
   But batch normalization does more than that:.
    ▶ Simply zero-centering and scaling by the standard deviation as
       explained on the previous slide would change what the layer can
       represent. For example, if we normalize the inputs of the sigmoid,
       then we would always be working in the linear domain of the sigmoid
       (remember that the sigmoid is only non-linear at very low or very
       high values) and therefore the neural network model would not be
       able to represent non-linear functions.
    ▶ Ioffe and Szegedy’s solution is to ensure that ‘the transformation
       inserted in the network can represent the identity transform’.
       In other words, the neural network needs to be given the chance to
       undo the normalization transformation entirely if it determines that
       this is the optimal thing to do. Most often this is not the optimal
       thing to do. What is often optimal is to change the transformation
       slightly.
    ▶ The latter is accomplished by applying, to the normalized inputs x̂, a
       subsequent operation γ x̂ + β, where γ and β are a sort of standard
       deviation and mean (also called the scale and shift parameter), but
       these are learned by the model itself with backpropagation.
                                                                               64 / 105

    ▶ The normalization is applied to each node k independently. The
       usual output of the node is here denoted by x (k) . If we have a

Batch Normalization - Making Predictions

    ▶ We now know that Batch Normalization calculates the mean and
      variance for each mini-batch at training time. But, what to do at
      testing (prediction) time? It would not be proper if we are to use
      mean and variance of the testing mini-batch, because it may be
      skewed. And what do we do if we predict for a single instance?
    ▶ What we do is, we calculate the population average of the mean and
      variances across all the training mini-batches, and at testing time,
      we use these two values in the normalization. This provides a more
      accurate value of the mean and variance.
    ▶ To calculate the population averages of the mini-batch mean and
      variances we keep a moving average so that we do not need to store
      all the mini-batch means and variances. This is explained in the
      following slide.

   vector σB2 (one σB2 for each input) are computed on every mini-batch and
   used in the exponentially weighted ‘moving average’:

                          2       2

   Here α is the ‘momentum’ given to the previous moving statistic, around
   0.99.

   moving variance is initialized from one.

Batch Normalization Performance

   Benefits of batch normalization, summarized:
    ▶ Batch Normalization considerably improves deep neural nets by
       strongly reducing the vanishing gradient problem. It makes the
       networks much less sensitive to initialization (even saturating
       functions can be used)
    ▶ It regularizes the network helping overfitting prevention
    ▶ When you start using batch normalization you will notice that it
      slows down training and that is because it needs to train extra
      parameters (Scale and Offset) for each node in a layer that is batch
      normalized. However, the batch normalization will actually speed up
      learning because substantially less training steps will be required to
      reach the same model performance because larger learning rates can
      be used. Each epoch will be slower but we will have to do less
      epochs.

Early Stopping
    ▶ Validation error can be used to detect when overfitting starts
    ▶ The goal is to stop training once validation error reaches a minimum.
      This is called early stopping because we are stopping training
      before the training loss has converged. This is not a simple task, as
      we only know when we have reached a minimum once validation
      error starts increasing, and the error curve is not always smooth.
    ▶ One solution is to use patience; the number of epochs after which
      the validation error has remained above the minimum, before
      stopping training, and then rolling back the model parameters to the
      point where the validation error was at a minimum. The patience is
      often set somewhere between 10 and 20, but it really depends on the
      dataset and network.

L-1 and L-2 Regularization
   LASSO, short for Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator, is a
   penalty added to the loss, and equal to the sum of the absolute value of
   all n weights (i.e., the L1 norm):

                                                  X
                            loss(x, y , θ) + λ            | θi |          (20)
                                                   i=1

   Ridge (L2), is a penalty added to the loss, and equal to the sum of all n
   weights squared (i.e., the L2 norm):

                                                                   2

===== PDF PAGE 90 =====
L-1 and L-2 Regularization
   Details:
    ▶ The Ridge (L2) loss is multiplied by 12 because the derivative of θ2 is
       2θ. This means in derivative space every weight counts as λθ, as
       opposed to λ2θ (there is no reason to have the 2 in there).
    ▶ Penalties added to the loss are sensitive to the scale of the data. For
       example, a variable with a larger scale will have a smaller weight,
       and thus that variable will be penalized less. When using penalties,
       inputs should always be scaled.
    ▶ In L1 (LASSO), weights shrink by a constant amount, whereas in L2
       (Ridge) the weights shrink by an amount proportional to the
       weights. When weights are large, L1 shrinks the weights a lot less
       than L2. When weights are small, L1 shrinks weights a lot more. For
       example, if an initial weight is small, 0.1, then L2 will grow by only
       0.01 while L1 grows by 0.1. If the initial weight is 3, then L1 will
       grow by only 3, while L2 will grow by 9. Therefore, L1 is said to be
       sparser than L2. Small weights (those between -1 and 1) are
       penalized disproportionately little in L2 compared to weights outside
       that range, while in L1 they are penalized proportionally. This means
       that in L1 weights are sparser (they arrive at zero faster) than in L2
       (it is much harder to get them to be zero).

Dropout
   ▶ Dropout (Srivastava, Hinton, et al, 2014) is very popular because it
     works so well and is simple to understand.
   ▶ At every training step, every neuron (input neurons and hidden
     neurons, but not output neurons) has a probability p of being
     temporarily dropped out. This means that it will be completely
     ignored (it outputs 0) during that training step, but it may be active
     during the next step.
   ▶ A typical dropout rate is between 10% and 50%.
   ▶ After training, neurons are not dropped anymore.
   ▶ Note that dropout slows down convergence, but results in better
     models.
   ▶ ORIGINAL PAPER:
     https://www.cs.toronto.edu/∼hinton/absps/JMLRdropout.pdf

Why does Dropout work?
  Explanation 1: ensembling
   ▶ A unique neural network is generated at each training step. Since
      each neuron can be either present or absent, there are a total of 2n
      (2 for either present or absent) possible ’thinned’ networks where n
      is the total number of droppable neurons. These networks are not
      independent since they share many of their weights but they are all
      different.
   ▶ Therefore, while implementing dropout, we are training many
      different networks, each with different (but shared) parameters. And
      when it is time to make predictions (dropout is only active at
      training time), the resulting network roughly represents the
      combination of all these networks which nearly always improves the
      performance of machine learning methods.
   ▶ Example: If we would train a network with 100,000 training
      iterations we have 100,000 different networks
   ▶ This can be perceived of as an averaging ensemble of all these
      smaller networks.

===== PDF PAGE 95 =====
Why does Dropout work?
  Explanation 2: resilience
   ▶ Consider a soccer team with 11 players. Some players specialize
      playing in the front, some in the middle, and some in the back.
   ▶ For every game we would flip a coin for each of the players, to
      decide if they can play or not. This means that if we would do that
      many times we would have 5 to 6 players in the team every game.
   ▶ In the last game the key front player was not allowed to play, so
      another player had to play that position and while doing so,
      gathered valuable experience, which they would have never learned if
      all 11 players were playing. The team has therefore become much
      more resilient.
   ▶ It is unclear if this system would work well in soccer, but it does
      work really well in neural networks. Neurons cannot co-adapt with
      neighboring neurons, and they cannot rely excessively on a few input
      neurons (since any input neuron can also be dropped out). This
      makes neurons less sensitive to small changes in the inputs, which
      reduces overfitting.

Important Detail
    ▶ Remember that dropout is on during training, and off during
      predicting
    ▶ If neurons are retained with probability 50% during training (the
      keep probability, or 1 - dropout probability), then every neuron will
      be connected to twice as many input neurons during prediction as it
      was on average during training.
    ▶ To compensate, during prediction after training, we need to
      multiply all the incoming weights of each neuron by 50% (the
      keep probability), otherwise the signal will be twice as large during
      prediction as what the network was trained on.
    ▶ Alternatively, we can divide each neuron’s output by the keep
      probability during training. In other words, there will be less weights
      and therefore we boost the output. TensorFlow does this for us when
      using tf.keras.layers.Dropout; we do not need to do this manually.
    ▶ In sum, during prediction double (if keep probability was 50%) the
      weights will be active and so we need to either halve the signal by
      multiplying weights by 50% during prediction, or double the signal
      during training

Practical Guidelines of Dropout
   Do not apply dropout
    ▶ right after the last layer, because the network has no ability to
       correct for dropout in subsequent layers.
    ▶ when the network is small relative to the dataset, regularization is
       usually unnecessary. If the model capacity is already low, lowering it
       further by adding regularization will hurt performance.
    ▶ when training time is limited. If we don’t train until convergence
       (e.g., we use early stopping), dropout may give worse results. Usually
       dropout hurts performance at the start of training, but results in the
       final ”converged” error being lower. Therefore, if we don’t plan to
       train until convergence, we may not want to use dropout.
   Other things to try:
    ▶ Increase the dropout rate when the model is overfitting, and
       decrease it when it is underfitting
    ▶ Add dropout only after the last (few) hidden layers in case of
       underfitting

    ▶ See dropout.py for a complete example. Each dropout layer drops
      some neurons (sets them to 0) and divides the remaining neurons’
      outputs by the keep probability
    ▶ In 2016, Gal and Ghahramani made a modification to dropout
      during the prediction phase called Monte Carlo Dropout (MC
      Dropout) that was shown to improve prediction performance. They
      also made a profound connection to approximate Bayesian Inference
      and ensembling.
    ▶ Their simple suggestion was to turn dropout on during prediction,
      and deploy the model k times on each record, resulting in k
      predictions per record. These k predictions can then be averaged
      (and we can also compute their standard deviation, for example).
      They showed that this average was a better prediction than making
      a single prediction on each record with dropout turned off.
    ▶ MC Dropout can be accomplished by tricking TensorFlow that it is in
      training mode, thereby activating the dropout layers. The following

Data Augmentation



   ▶ One last regularization technique is data augmentation.
   ▶ It consists of generating new training instances from existing ones,
     artificially boosting the size of the training set.
   ▶ This will reduce overfitting. The trick is to generate realistic training
     instances. Ideally a human should not be able to tell which instances
     were generated and which ones were not.
   ▶ Adding simply white noise will not help because it is not learnable.
   ▶ Mostly used in Convolutional Neural Networks (see later).
   ▶ Instances can be generated on the fly during training

Deep Neural Networks

    ▶ In recent years, neural networks have become deeper and deeper,
      with state-of-the-art networks going from 7 layers (AlexNet) to 1000
      layers (Residual Nets) in the space of 4 years.
    ▶ However, the number of units in the fully-connected layers, has
      remained roughly the same and is still relatively small in magnitude,
      despite the large increase in the number of layers.
    ▶ The advantage of multiple layers is that they attempt to model
      various levels of abstractions in data by using architectures
      composed of multiple non-linear transformations.
    ▶ The idea draws from how animals and humans learn: with simpler
      concepts earlier in life and higher-level abstractions later, expressed
      in terms of the previously learned concepts.
    ▶ Multiple layers are much better at generalizing because they learn all
      the intermediate features between the raw data and the high-level
      classification.

Wide Neural Networks

   Then, how about going wider (i.e., more neurons, less layers)?
    ▶ The advantage of wide neural networks is that the model will be
       very good at memorization. Memorization can be loosely defined as
       learning the frequent co-occurrence of items or features and
       exploiting the correlation available in the historical data.
    ▶ Widening consistently improves performance across networks of
       different depth.
    ▶ Increasing width helps until the number of parameters becomes too
       large and stronger regularization is needed.
    ▶ Wider networks allow many multiplications to be computed in
       parallel, while deeper networks use more sequential computations
       (since the computations depend on the previous layer). Modern
       GPUs are better suited for parallel computing
    ▶ Increasing depth results in better generalization; exploring new
       feature combinations that have never or rarely occurred in the past.
